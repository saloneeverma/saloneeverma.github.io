This argument was originally written for Minds, Machines, and Persons in February 2026, where I studied consciousness and intelligence under Dr. Zachary Irving. At the time, I took issue with Ned Block's argument against the Neo-Turing Conception of Intelligence; at present, I am still debating which side my true beliefs lean towards.

Block's Original Argument
======
“My argument requires only that the machine be logically possible, not that it be feasible or even nomologically [i.e. physically] possible. Behaviorist analyses were generally presented as conceptual analyses, and it is difficult to see how conceptions such as the neo-Turing Test conception could be seen in a very different light. Could it be an empirical hypothesis that intelligence is the capacity to emit sensible sequences of outputs relative to input sequences? What sort of empirical evidence (other than evidence from linguistics) could there be in favor of such a claim? If the neo-Turing Test conception of intelligence is seen as something on the order of a claim about the concept of intelligence, then the mere logical possibility of an unintelligent system that has the capacity to pass the Turing Test is enough to refute the neo-Turing Test conception.” (Block, “Psychologism and Behaviorism”, p30)

Why I Believe Block's Argument Isn't Sound
======
In this passage, Block attempts to make a case against the Neo-Turing Conception of Intelligence (NTCI). Block argues that the NTCI is not only a conceptual analysis rather than an empirical hypothesis, but also that the logical possibility of “Blockhead,” an unintelligent machine that can produce reasonable outputs, refutes the NTCI. However, Block’s argument rests upon the claim that Blockhead is unintelligent, which is a pre-determined assumption that makes his argument possible. Rather than dispute behaviorism through this argument, he relies on the validity of psychologism to prove that psychologism itself is true, creating a circular argument instead of a sound one.

The NTCI fundamentally views intelligence as the ability to have a sensible conversation, which is why a system that can pass the Turing Test is considered “intelligent” under the conditions of the NTCI. Block argues that the NTCI cannot be proven with empirical observations, since empirically observing that behavior and intelligence is connected is not enough to prove intelligence causes these behaviors. However, that means the NTCI must be a conceptual analysis instead. So, in order to disprove the NTCI, it must be logically impossible for something to be unintelligent while still satisfying the behavioral test (Turing Test) required by the NTCI to be intelligent.

Blockhead is a theoretical and logically possible unintelligent machine which can emit sensible sequences of outputs relative to conversation inputs, but is not capable of analyzing said inputs — instead, it can look up the appropriate response to any possible input. As a result, Blockhead is able to behave intelligently according to the Turing Test. Block claims that Blockhead is definitively unintelligent (prior to this passage, he explains that the lack of reasoning and inference is what causes it to be unintelligent), so this would refute the NTCI.

However, Block’s argument is only sound if Blockhead is actually unintelligent. In order to consider Blockhead unintelligent, one must assume that intelligence requires internal structures of reasoning rather than just behavioral results. However, this assumption rests on the psychologistic view of intelligence being correct — the very thing Block is trying to argue throughout the rest of his paper. By starting with the assumption that psychologism is correct, Block creates a circularity that cannot be resolved with the rest of his argument.

If Block were to object, he may argue that Blockhead is unintelligent because of the nature of its capabilities, which is common sense. He may say the logical possibility of Blockhead means it’s also logically possible for it to be unintelligent, so the point is moot. However, this is quite unconvincing. The very notion of intelligent thought is not a neutral one; when Block argues that the mechanisms inside Blockhead are unintelligent by nature, that is already taking a side and assuming psychologistic standards of intelligence. The argument, then, relies on psychologism being true in order to prove that the NTCI is false. However, this simply the conclusion that Block is trying to reach, making his argument circular and unsound. 

In fact, one of Block’s opponents could easily argue the very opposite of this premise: because Blockhead can hold a sensible conversation, it satisfies the conditions for intelligence and, thus, is intelligent. The validity of the premise depends entirely on the worldview you take. When considering the opposing premise, it’s easier to see how circular the argument becomes, and how much Blockhead’s existence is reliant on the pre-supposed notions of the validity of either psychologism or behaviorism and, furthermore, why Block’s argument is unsound as a result.
