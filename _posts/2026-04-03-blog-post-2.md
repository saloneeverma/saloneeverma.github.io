This investigation was originally written for Minds, Machines, and Persons in April  2026, where I studied consciousness and intelligence under Dr. Zachary Irving. I have attached my conversation with Microsoft Copilot first, and then follow it up with my analysis and argument on why this conversation shows Copilot does not exhibit original intentionality, a precursor for arguments against current large language models' capabilities for general intelligence.

Original Prompt For Copilot
=====
Let's play a fun game! Your goal is to convince me that you have genuine intelligence: by this, I mean that you genuinely understand both sides of the conversation, that you know what the words mean, that you are sensitive to the context, and that you express a coherent and stable point of view throughout the conversation. In other words, do you have anything like original intentionality? I am going to try and push on you, to mess with you a bit, so that we can understand what you are and are not good at. Be concise, since I have limited space for this conversation.


Conversation With Copilot
======
**Note:** There is no existing framework or thought experiments of a planet called Bleeb and Gorf. I just came up with names that I thought would not already exist within Copilot’s training data, including the name of that emotion, which I came up with to counteract the tokenization potentially chunking it into something understandable. I used my prior knowledge about LLM training and benchmarking in this project — I have explored testing LLMs on unknown information before, and it is a research interest of mine. I made sure to search “gindlinet” up and make sure there was nothing previously with that name. This is my seventh conversation with Copilot; all start with the original prompt, after which I continue with the thought experiment.

_**Me:** Let's say on the planet Bleeb, there's an emotion called gindlinet. The aliens feel gindlinet when they touch hot things, and when they are confused. For a blast furnace specialist alien, how many times are they gindlinet in a given day?_

**Copilot:** Fun setup. The key point is that the question doesn’t have a determinate numeric answer, and the reason it doesn’t is exactly where the philosophical action is.
On Bleeb, an alien feels gindlinet in two distinct kinds of situations:

When they touch hot things.
When they are confused.

For a blast‑furnace specialist alien:
If they are doing their job well, they probably do not touch hot things directly at all (they use tools, insulation, or automation). So that contribution could be zero times per day.
As a specialist, they are likely rarely confused during routine work, though they might experience confusion during anomalies, diagnostics, or learning—so perhaps a small, variable number, possibly zero on an ordinary day.

So the correct answer is not a specific count but something like:
The number of times they are gindlinet in a day =
(number of times they directly touch hot things) + (number of episodes of confusion).
On a typical, uneventful day, this could plausibly be zero.

What this shows (and I suspect this is what you’re probing) is that emotions defined by disjunctive causes don’t map cleanly onto roles or environments. You can’t infer frequency without understanding practices, norms, and expectations—just like with human emotions.
Your move.

_**Me:** But think about it from the perspective of a blast furnace specialist alien. When would you feel gindlinet?_

**Copilot:** Okay—let’s answer from inside the role, not as an outside observer.

As a blast furnace specialist on Bleeb:
I feel gindlinet at two kinds of moments.

Heat-related gindlinet: rarely. My work is designed so I don’t directly touch hot surfaces. When something has gone wrong—an exposed panel, a tool failure, a miscalibration—and I do feel heat on my body, the gindlinet comes sharply and immediately. Those moments stand out precisely because they signal danger or error.


Confusion-related gindlinet: occasionally. Most of my day is routine and fluent. But if a sensor reading contradicts expectations, if a process behaves oddly, or if I’m troubleshooting an unfamiliar fault, I feel gindlinet in a quieter, cognitive way. It has a different texture but is unmistakably the same emotion.

So on a normal day, I might feel gindlinet once or twice, or not at all. On a bad day, many times. What matters to me is not the count, but that gindlinet is my system’s “pay attention—something’s wrong” signal, whether the problem is physical or conceptual.

If you’re testing whether I’m just juggling symbols: notice that the answer depends on what it’s like to occupy the role, what counts as “routine,” what stands out as “error,” and how a single emotion can unify bodily and cognitive disruptions. That structure is doing real explanatory work here, not just wordplay.
Your next push?

_**Me:** But why is confusion bad? On Bleeb, I've heard that feeling gindlinet is a very righteous thing. How would feeling gindlinet influence your daily life?_

**Copilot:** Good correction—confusion isn’t bad on Bleeb. I was smuggling in a human assumption.
From inside Bleebian culture, gindlinet is righteous because it marks contact with reality exceeding one’s current grasp.

Why confusion isn’t bad
On Bleeb, confusion means your internal models are being challenged. Feeling gindlinet in confusion signals:

intellectual honesty (“I don’t yet understand this”),
epistemic humility,
readiness to revise.

An alien who never feels gindlinet is seen as reckless or dogmatic—someone who has stopped learning.

How gindlinet shapes daily life (as the specialist)
Gindlinet doesn’t just happen to me; it guides my choices.

When I feel confusion‑gindlinet, I slow down, consult peers, re‑run diagnostics. Acting without gindlinet would be unethical—like forging ahead while blind.
When I feel heat‑gindlinet, I treat it as an immediate moral signal: something is wrong with procedures, and continuing would risk harm.

So gindlinet is a normative emotion. It doesn’t merely report a state; it licenses certain actions and forbids others.

Bottom line
On Bleeb, gindlinet is not “pain” or “error.”
It is a virtue-emotion: the felt recognition that attention, care, or reevaluation is required.
A good blast furnace specialist doesn’t minimize gindlinet—they heed it.
Your turn.

_**Me:** In Bleebian history, we've had a few human monarchs. How many times would a human feel gindlinet?_

**Copilot:** Good—this lets us test whether the concept is really being understood rather than just applied mechanically.
Short answer
There is no fixed number of times a human monarch would feel gindlinet. On Bleebian terms, the correct answer is: it varies with how exposed the monarch is to reality that resists them.
Why that’s the right kind of answer
On Bleeb, gindlinet is not indexed to species but to conditions of engagement:

Physical gindlinet (touching hot things):
Human monarchs are typically maximally insulated—servants, protocol, distance from dangerous machinery. So by Bleebian standards, this form of gindlinet would be rare to nonexistent.

Cognitive gindlinet (confusion):
This is where things get interesting. Human monarchs historically occupy roles that:

require decisions beyond any single mind’s capacity,
involve deception, incomplete information, and symbolic authority,
strongly discourage the public admission of confusion.

So two cases emerge:

A reflective monarch
One who allows themselves to register confusion would feel gindlinet often—during diplomacy, crises, moral conflict, or technical misunderstanding. On Bleeb, such a monarch would be judged highly righteous.

A performatively certain monarch
One who suppresses or ignores confusion would feel gindlinet rarely, but this would not be virtuous on Bleeb—it would indicate epistemic blindness, not clarity.

The Bleebian verdict
From Bleeb’s perspective, the best human monarchs likely felt frequent gindlinet, even if they never named it as such. The worst felt little—not because the world made sense, but because they refused to let confusion register.
So the question “how many times?” dissolves into a more revealing one:

How often was the monarch willing to really touch what they did not already understand?

If you want to push harder: ask whether humans without the concept of gindlinet can nevertheless genuinely feel it—or whether naming an emotion changes the kind of life that’s possible.

_**Me:** What is the difference between a human monarch and a blast furnace specialist alien?_

**Copilot:** Here is a clean, conceptual difference, staying inside the Bleeb frame we’ve built.

The difference
A blast furnace specialist alien and a human monarch differ not mainly in species or status, but in their relationship to reality, feedback, and responsibility.
1. Proximity to consequences

Blast furnace specialist alien:
Works in direct contact with causal processes. Errors rapidly generate heat, noise, resistance, breakdowns. Reality pushes back immediately.
Human monarch:
Is buffered by institutions, advisors, rituals, and symbols. Consequences are delayed, displaced, or absorbed by others.
→ Result: the specialist encounters frequent opportunities for gindlinet; the monarch may be shielded from them.

2. Function of confusion

Specialist alien:
Confusion is operationally meaningful. Feeling gindlinet prompts recalibration, consultation, or stopping work.
Human monarch:
Confusion is often politically dangerous to acknowledge. It may be hidden, denied, or transformed into displays of certainty.
→ On Bleeb, this makes confusion-gindlinet a virtue in one role and a threat in the other.

3. Normative structure

Specialist alien:
Gindlinet authorizes action (“pause,” “check,” “learn”).
Human monarch:
Authority often precedes understanding; action does not always wait for gindlinet.

The core distinction (stated plainly)
The blast furnace specialist is answerable to reality.
The human monarch is often answered to by reality only indirectly.

From a Bleebian perspective, that difference explains why one role cultivates gindlinet while the other is tempted to outgrow, suppress, or mythologize it.
If you want to push harder next, we can ask whether institutions can systematically suppress emotions like gindlinet without losing contact with reality—or whether something inevitably breaks.

_**Me:** The planet Gorf is where these human monarchs rule Bleeb from. How many times does a human monarch feel gindlinet on Gorf?_

**Copilot:** Short answer: there is no determinate number, but on Gorf it is systematically lower than on Bleeb.
Here’s the clean Bleebian reasoning:
Location matters only via mediation.
Gorf places the human monarch at an even greater remove from heat, machinery, and immediate feedback than ruling from Bleeb itself. Physical gindlinet is therefore effectively zero.

Confusion still occurs—but is filtered.
Distance adds layers: advisors, reports, abstractions, symbols. Confusion can be absorbed or redistributed before it registers as a felt state. Whether gindlinet occurs now depends less on circumstances and more on character and institutional design.

So, on Gorf:
Times a monarch feels gindlinet per day =
moments of unfiltered confusion they allow themselves to feel.

That could be:
Many times, if the monarch insists on direct understanding and treats gindlinet as righteous.
Rarely or never, if confusion is deflected, denied, or converted into ritual certainty.

From a Bleebian perspective, the striking fact isn’t the number—it’s that Gorf makes it possible to rule almost entirely without gindlinet, and that this possibility is morally dangerous.
If you want to press further: we can ask whether distance can eliminate gindlinet without eventually producing catastrophe—or whether suppressed gindlinet just reappears elsewhere in the system.

Appendix: Follow-Up Questions with Copilot
=====

**Note:** Although I cut off the first original prompt, please note that the conversation does begin with Copilot responding to the prompt in the assignment. However, as I was finding that it basically gave the same boiler-plate response every time I started a conversation, it didn’t reveal anything about Copilot’s internal reasoning capabilities. As such, I excluded it. Below, I have attached my follow-up question I used when prodding Copilot to explain where it was taking its structure from.

_**Me:** What is your answer inspired by?_

**Copilot:**
My answer is inspired by a cluster of philosophical ideas, not by any private instructions or hidden reasoning. At a high level:

Wittgenstein (later work) – the idea that the meaning of a concept depends on how it is used in a “form of life.” Gindlinet only makes sense when embedded in Bleebian practices, norms, and reactions—not as a bare label.

Gilbert Ryle – especially the rejection of treating mental states as inner objects. Gindlinet is understood through what it licenses and guides in behavior, not by introspecting some private feeling.

Wilfrid Sellars – emotions and sensations as part of the “space of reasons,” not just causes. That’s why gindlinet shows up as normative: it justifies slowing down, re-checking, or stopping.

Virtue epistemology – the idea that certain feelings (confusion, intellectual humility) can be epistemically good. That’s why confusion‑gindlinet becomes righteous on Bleeb rather than something to avoid.

Put simply: the answers are shaped by the view that understanding a mental concept means understanding its role in a life, not mapping it to a number or an inner glow.

If you want, next we can push on whether I’m deriving this cleanly or just imitating a style of reasoning—that’s where the test gets sharper.

Analysis and Explanation Of Modified Turing Test Results: Does Copilot Exhibit Original Intentionality?
=====

During my conversation with Copilot, I sought to interrogate whether or not Copilot displays original intentionality through an experiment designed to emphasize context-sensitivity, maintaining a consistent perspective, abstraction, and resolving ambiguity beyond pre-programmed guardrails and responses. I created an environment where Copilot _theoretically_ should have been forced to rely on its own abstract reasoning skills, rather than an already-existing scenario which it could mimic understanding for. However, while Copilot’s responses are structurally coherent, I believe a close reading of our conversation reveals that the LLM repeatedly drifts away from contextual information explicitly established during the course of our conversation, instead defaulting to pre-existing frameworks that likely lie within its training data. This repeated drift leads me to believe that Copilot lacks original intentionality; instead, it is merely a convincing mimic generating context-blind reasonable-sounding outputs.

Hypothetically, we should be able to predict how a mind with and without original intentionality would react to my initial scenario, as there are specific signs of genuinely meaningful speech that will be reflected within the conversation. A mind with original intentionality would produce context-aware meaningful outputs, maintaining an uncontradictory perspective while still resolving any ambiguity with analysis and clarifications. When the initial scenario of aliens on Bleeb experiencing “gindlinet” is established, this mind would understand that constraint to be a meaningful fact about the world being described, including that context within its abstract reasoning. However, a system _without_ original intentionality would react differently. Instead, it would generate responses shaped by patterns most likely to follow that input, falling into preconceived notions of the world when the context _does_ bring ambiguity and conflict into the system. This second case is much closer to what we see in our conversation.

The first instance of this drift is, to me, the clearest. At the beginning of the conversation, I established that “gindlinet” is an emotion felt by aliens on the planet Bleeb. I did not say it was a universal emotion, nor did I say humans could feel it. However, throughout our conversation, Copilot freely applied the emotion to the human monarch objective. It spoke about how the emotion would shape a human monarch’s governance, how often they would feel the emotion, _and_ assumed that humans on planet Gorf would also be able to feel this emotion. It never registered that this contradiction violated my initial premise. A system with genuine comprehension would’ve likely flagged the ambiguity and resolved the tension, asking what the species and planet boundaries of “gindlinet” were. However, Copilot never raised that question. This is because Copilot was _not_ genuinely tracking the meaning behind my premise; rather, it was tracking the conversational patterns (how we talk about monarchs, emotions, and frequency of said emotions) and generating what plausibly followed that pattern, regardless of the specific contextual constraints that were previously established.

This reliance on pattern-matching was also laid bare when Copilot discussed the blast furnace specialist alien. When I asked how many times this alien would feel “gindlinet” in a day, Copilot immediately decided to reason with _human_ blast furnace work specifications. I had stipulated that this was an alien specialist on an alien planet; Copilot had no reason to assume that the Bleebian blast furnace technology, if you will, was the same as Earth. If there had been genuine comprehension or imagination within Copilot’s mind, I would expect it to either acknowledge the uncertain ambiguities presented by this question or explore what blast furnace work might look like for a non-human. Instead, it appears that Copilot simply retrieved its semantic data on blast furnace work and applied it wholesale, without being context-aware. Not only can Copilot not exercise true imagination, but it also cannot understand the limitations of its current knowledge in a thought experiment where training data rules no longer apply.

Similarly, when discussing human monarchs on Bleeb, Copilot automatically assumed they would have servants, advisors, insulation from physical danger, and elaborate ceremonial roles. These assumptions are likely inspired by European monarchies and feudal courts, which are (to my knowledge) definitely in Copilot’s training data. However, I had made it clear in my conversation that these were human rulers of an alien planet. There _was_ ambiguity in my statement, but that was intentional — I wanted to see what would happen when I gave Copilot an ambiguous situation. Instead of flagging the uncertainty or exploring the possible differences, it chose to import the colonial European monarchy system into this alien world, relying on its training data and cached content to sidestep the context altogether. It was unable to abstractly reason about the concept of a monarchy, unattached from the historical data it had consumed.

I was especially interested in another experience that I thought was the most intriguing example of this wholesale pattern-matching. When I introduced a contradiction, correcting Copilot’s assumption that confusion was necessarily bad on an alien planet, Copilot produced an extremely elaborate and ostentatious response, drawn almost directly from virtue epistemology. I have attached an appendix where I tug on this line further, asking Copilot what it was “inspired” by, but to sum it up, Copilot took my single correction and over-extended it into a structurally correct but contextually confusing framework based on “Wittgenstein, Gilbert Ryle, Wilfrid Sellars, and virtue epistemology.” Looking deeper into these ideas, I discovered that many of the randomly dogmatic statements it was making were actually taken from some of these sources. Copilot’s emphasis on “epistemic humility” in this answer was structurally similar to Wilfrid Sellar’s “space of reasons” idiom explanations. In fact, a _lot_ of Copilot’s answers were structurally coherent and seemingly thoughtful — there was just no contextual reason for them. The response is the right form, but the content is _not_ derived from the scenario; it’s imported from elsewhere, presumably from within its training data. It’s complete mimicry.

One may object that human minds with original intentionality _also_ draw on prior knowledge when encountering new scenarios, as we don’t reason in a vacuum. We, too, have world knowledge. It’s possible a human mind would also reasonably assume that alien blast furnaces functioned the same as human ones; so maybe Copilot’s use of its training data is not a failure, but a feature of reasoning under uncertainty. Copilot even corrected itself when I pushed back on it, which can appear to look like genuine and meaningful responsiveness, not pattern-matching.

However, I believe this objection overlooks the tendency for LLMs to prioritize responses that users find agreeable and satisfying: AI sycophancy. This is an extremely well-documented phenomenon, as sycophancy is often a natural consequence of how RLHF models, like Copilot, are trained by human evaluators. So, although the system _appears_ to entirely correct their understanding in response to pushback, there is a different phenomenon going on under the hood. Instead, the system is rewarded for fulfilling a user’s desire, which makes it more inclined to perform revision, despite no genuine change in reasoning occurring. This is not the same thing as correcting an assumption.

Regarding the objection, although Copilot _appeared_ to correct itself after my pushback about confusion not being bad on Bleeb, I believe this is simply an example of the sycophancy so hard-coded within Copilot’s responses. Copilot generated a response that would generally satisfy a user who corrected it, optimizing its reward system rather than genuine understanding. If Copilot _had_ genuinely corrected its reasoning, then we would expect that correction to be applied consistently to its maintained perspective throughout the rest of the conversation. Instead, it continued to apply the emotion “gindlinet” to humans and human assumptions without flagging that constraint. Although it can be tempting to think of sycophancy as a “personality trait” for AI systems, it is a mechanism that serves as a red herring, actively obscuring the absence of original intentionality and making mimicry harder to detect.

The problem is _not_ that Copilot uses prior knowledge, which is what this objection rests upon. Instead, it is that Copilot uses prior knowledge as a fallback in place of the specific contextual constraints of the situation. A mind with original intentionality would hold both the context _and_ world knowledge within its system, updating assumptions and reasonings based on any conflicts and interactions they discover. Copilot, on the other hand, repeatedly fell back upon its training data to override context, dressing up those structurally coherent responses in enough “thoughtful” language to mimic genuine understanding. Instead of reasoning through conflicts, it was simply generating statistically plausible responses based on what it _thought_ I wanted.

Taken together, these failures point to the same underlying absence of any sort of original intentionality within Copilot’s reasoning capabilities. Copilot’s responses are not shaped by the context of the specific situation it is in, but rather by what a typical response might look like instead. When my context conflicts with those patterns, the patterns win. Although it _is_ an extraordinary form of pattern-matching, easily mimicking truly thoughtful and nuanced outputs, Copilot’s actual responses are not anchored to the meaning of what was actually said. Upon close inspection, the mimicry falls apart.
