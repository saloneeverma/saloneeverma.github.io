When you think of AI art, how do you feel? Fear? Annoyance? Rage? Optimism? Joy? Chances are, you feel pretty strongly towards AI-generated art, whether that's a positive emotion or a negative emotion. Chances _also_ are that this is a kneejerk reaction. It's understandable; most people feel very strongly about what they perceive as the "human" quality in a piece of art. Our team wanted to explore this social and technical tension.

We want to argue that the process of how people come to their own judgement on whether AI art is "real" or not matters more than what actually constitutes “real” art. The process of forming, challenging, and reforming a judgement is a valuable exercise, and we want to encourage these critical processes.

How Do People Find Value In Art?
=====

When it comes to understanding people's reactions to art, we wanted to take a more holistic view of artistic consumption experiences. So many people feel a really strong resistance to AI involvement in art, and when trying to understand the source of that resistance, we came to realize we needed to understand how different people experience art.

[Keith Holyoak](https://reasoninglab.psych.ucla.edu/keith-holyoak/) from the UCLA Reasoning Lab is a poet and psychologist who combines behavioral investigations with cognitive neuroscience and computational models of cognition. Holyoak has often argued that AI-generated art lacks inner experience and, thus, lacks "what is most needed to place the footprints of its own consciousness on another mind." If you take Holyoak's view of art, many people experience art through their inner experience with the perceived author. Art is shaped by our memories, emotions, and lived experiences - many people take the view that although AI can create things that look like art, it doesn't have enough personal depth to be real art.

Similarly, we looked at what [Ted Chiang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ted_Chiang), an American science fiction writer and frequent non-fiction contributor to the New Yorker, has to say about art. We were struck by his emphasis on art as a series of intentional choices; he believes the "countless small-scale choices made during implementation are just as important to the final product as the few large-scale choices made during the conception." For followers of this school of artistic consumption, art reflects a process of human decision-making at every level that is valued more than the final product _or_ the initial idea.

Lastly, we looked at what [David Cope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Cope), a pioneer in combining AI and music, believed about experiencing art. We were intrigued by his focus on the viewer's role in art, and I was particularly interested in his most famous quote: "the soul I hear, if indeed I hear one, is my own." He suggests that the meaning we derive from a piece of art actually comes from us, as the viewers, rather than the artist themselves.

What Do Real People Believe?
=====

This is what experts had to say; we wanted to know what non-experts with less developed artistic experience processes had to say on this question. We interviewed eleven people 20-60 years old, with varying amounts of AI familiarity. We asked them a series of questions about beliefs on AI and art, and led them through an activity, often called an AI Art Turing Test. Generally, we showed them a couple pieces of art, both human and AI-generated, and we had them discuss whether or not they thought the piece was AI-generated and why. Only after they explained their thought process would we reveal the truth.

We found that 64% of participants reported some shift in their thinking, but it's more interesting that the beliefs that changed were about _detectability_, not their core beliefs about AI art. Some were surprised by how difficult it was to detect AI-generated art, and some became even more protective of human artists after the activity. They emphasized labeling, effort, and authorship as their most important values.

We were surprised to find that confidence didn't predict actual performance. While some participants with high confidence scored well on the activity, others with the same level of confidence did extremely poorly. Similar results were found for participants with low confidence. When the participants did try to detect AI, they used strategies such as checking the brushstrokes, texture, shading, anatomy, and the uncanny valley qualities of the art. Increasingly, young participants focused on the "vibe" of the piece of art in question, taking a more holisitc view and determining authorship based on how it made them feel.

Our Artifact: Is AI Art Real Art? Website
=====

We wanted to replicate this experience of challenging assumptions and looking inwards for a wider audience. We wanted to create an easily accessible way to encourage people to explore and challenge their social, personal, and artistic values. Rather than answering the question “Is AI Art ‘Real’ Art?” we wanted to provide people with the tools to critically think about their own experiences with art and ownership, and to evaluate their own personal reasonings behind the values they hold. 

We wanted to accomplish a few specific aims with this website. First, we wanted to provide people with background knowledge on AI art, similar to a museum label or exhibition background card. We sought to mimic a museum exhibit structure with the website, because we thought the curation of our content would be more accessible when structured in a way people already experience art. We also wanted to provide them with demonstrations, to explain how some of these AI tools worked, which they can then play with themselves. Finally, we wanted to provide users with real perspectives from experts and our interviewees, especially ones that they may not have heard of before - we wanted to invoke critical thinking with a variety of diverse perspectives, rather than create an echo chamber. 

You can find the website at this address: [https://saloneeverma.github.io/ai-art-investigation/#](https://saloneeverma.github.io/ai-art-investigation/#). 

All the background cards are written by hand, and researched extensively to provide more information to our viewers. Check out the Turing Test activity to see if you can challenge your own beliefs as well!

Takeaways
=====

Ultimately, because this question sparks a kneejerk reaction, we’ve found it’s an effective vehicle in prompting people to identify their fixed assumptions. While we may not have answers about what true art is, we hope this project allows a wider audience to grapple with this question and their own beliefs.
